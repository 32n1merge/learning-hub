Lesson 2: The Model — A Pyramid of Dysfunction

Here is Lencioni's central claim, in his own words:

"Not finance. Not strategy. Not technology. It is teamwork that remains the ultimate competitive advantage, both because it is so powerful and so rare."

Teams fail not because their strategy is wrong or their talent is thin, but because of five predictable behavioral traps. The traps are conceptually simple — and brutally hard to fix, because fixing them requires people to change how they behave, not what they know.

What is a "team," anyway?

Lencioni borrows the classic definition: a team is a small number of people with complementary skills who are committed to a common purpose, performance goals, and approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable. If any of those pieces is missing — mutual accountability being the one most often missing — you have a group of people who report to the same boss, not a team.

The pyramid

The five dysfunctions stack. Absence of trust is the foundation; inattention to results is the apex. Each layer makes the layer above it possible:

Read the pyramid from the bottom up: the more severe and foundational the dysfunction, the more it breeds the ones above it. Fix the bottom, and the top becomes fixable. Ignore the bottom, and no amount of cheerleading about "results" will stick.

The five dysfunctions at a glance

DysfunctionWhat it looks likeRoot driver
1. Absence of trustPeople hide weaknesses and mistakes, won't ask for help, play politicsUnwillingness to be vulnerable
2. Fear of conflictArtificial harmony, boring meetings, back-channel gossip, decisions never truly testedDesire to preserve harmony
3. Lack of commitmentAmbiguity about priorities, re-litigating decisions, missed deadlinesLack of clarity and buy-in
4. Avoidance of accountabilityLow standards, the leader as sole disciplinarian, resentment of underperformersDucking interpersonal discomfort
5. Inattention to resultsEgo, status, and individual goals trump the team's goals; silosFocus on personal success

How the dysfunctions cascade

The model is a chain of causation. Each link:

  • No trust → no conflict. If you can't be vulnerable, you can't risk a passionate argument. Conflict feels personal and dangerous, so the team opts for fake harmony.
  • No conflict → no commitment. If ideas were never truly debated, nobody feels heard — and people only commit to decisions they helped shape. Without aired disagreement, "agreement" is feigned.
  • No commitment → no accountability. If goals and standards are fuzzy and nobody really bought in, nobody has the right — or the basis — to call anyone out.
  • No accountability → no results. If poor behavior and missed commitments go unremarked, individuals optimize for themselves. Ego fills the vacuum where collective standards should be.
The virtuous cycle is the same chain, run forward: trust enables healthy conflict → healthy conflict produces real buy-in → real buy-in gives everyone the standing to hold each other accountable → mutual accountability forces attention on collective results.

One more consequence of the model: because the dysfunctions compound, most struggling teams exhibit several of them at once. The most useful diagnostic question isn't "which dysfunction do we have?" — it's "where is the lowest one we have?" Because that's where the work has to start.
