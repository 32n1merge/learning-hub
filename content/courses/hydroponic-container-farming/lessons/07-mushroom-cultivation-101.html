🍄 Mushroom Cultivation 101

Mushrooms aren't plants. They don't photosynthesize. They don't need light to grow. Instead, they're decomposers — fungi that break down organic matter and absorb nutrients from their substrate. This fundamental difference means a mushroom container operates on completely different rules than a vegetable container. But that difference is exactly what makes them perfect partners.

Fungi 101: What we call a "mushroom" is actually the fruiting body of a much larger organism. The real fungus is the mycelium — a vast network of microscopic thread-like cells (hyphae) that colonize the substrate. The mushroom is just the reproductive structure, like an apple on a tree. The tree is the mycelium.

🎯 For a container farm: Start with oyster mushrooms — they're forgiving, fast, and grow on cheap substrates like pasteurized straw. Blue oyster, pink oyster, and golden oyster varieties offer visual appeal and distinct flavors. Once you've mastered oysters, add Lion's Mane (high market value, $12–20/lb retail).

Container Layout: The Mushroom Factory

Unlike the vegetable container's vertical NFT walls, the mushroom container uses stainless steel wire shelving (5–6 tiers per rack, 2–3 racks deep) to hold fruiting blocks. Key design elements:

  • Incubation zone (front 1/3): Warmer (70–75°F), dark, for colonizing blocks. Higher CO₂ is fine here — mycelium tolerates it.
  • Fruiting zone (rear 2/3): Cooler (55–65°F), humidity 85–95%, frequent air exchange to trigger pinning.
  • Humidification: Ultrasonic foggers or high-pressure misting systems maintain the 85–95% RH. A standard humidistat controls the cycle.
  • Sterile handling area: A small laminar flow hood or still-air box for inoculation work. Contamination is the #1 enemy.

The Substrate Question

Substrate is the mushroom's "soil" — the material mycelium colonizes and eventually fruits from. Each species has preferences:

  • Oyster mushrooms: Pasteurized wheat/rice straw (cheapest), supplemented sawdust, or even spent coffee grounds
  • Lion's Mane / Shiitake: Supplemented hardwood sawdust (oak, maple) — 80% sawdust + 20% wheat bran
  • Spent substrate: After 3–5 flushes, the substrate is depleted of nutrients the mushroom needs — but it's now a partially decomposed, nutrient-rich compost perfect for soil amendment. You can use it to grow soil-based crops or sell it as garden compost.
🔑 The nutrient loop: Mushroom spent substrate can be composted further and used as organic fertilizer for soil beds. Or, the CO₂ produced during mushroom respiration can be routed directly to the vegetable container. Two waste streams become valuable inputs.
