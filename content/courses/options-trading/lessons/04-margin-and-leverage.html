Lesson 4: Margin & Leverage

Margin is how retail traders punch above their weight — and how they get absolutely destroyed. It's the double-edged sword that makes options the most dangerous game in finance.

TL;DR: Margin lets you control more shares than you can actually afford. When it goes right, you look like a genius. When it goes wrong — and it will — you can lose more than you put in. Much more.

What is Margin?

Margin is a loan from your broker secured by the assets in your account. There are two key concepts:

1. Buying on Margin (Leverage)

You deposit $10,000. Your broker lets you buy up to $20,000 worth of stock (2:1 leverage for equities under Reg T). You borrow the other $10,000 and pay interest on it.

Example: You use $20,000 to buy 200 shares of a $100 stock with $10,000 of your own money + $10,000 borrowed. If the stock goes to $120, your position is worth $24,000. After repaying the $10,000 loan, your equity is $14,000 — a 40% return on your $10,000 instead of 20%. But if it drops to $80, your position is worth $16,000. After repaying $10,000, you have $6,000 — a −40% loss instead of −20%.

2. Initial Margin vs. Maintenance Margin

TermDefinitionReg T Standard
Initial MarginMinimum equity required to open a position50% of purchase price
Maintenance MarginMinimum equity you must maintain in your account25% (brokers can set higher — usually 30%)
Margin CallBroker demands more money or liquidates your positionsTriggered below maintenance

Options Margin Requirements

Options margin is different from stock margin. Here's what matters:

Option PositionMargin Required
Long calls/puts (buying)Premium paid only — no additional margin. Max loss is defined.
Naked short callMassive. Formula: Premium + max(20% of underlying − OTM amount, 10% of underlying). Potentially unlimited loss.
Naked short putPremium + max(20% of underlying − OTM amount, 10% of underlying). Loss capped at strike × 100.
Covered callNone beyond owning the shares. Shares serve as collateral.
Cash-secured putCash equal to strike × 100 must be held. No leverage.
Spread (defined risk)Width of the spread × 100. Defined and known upfront.
⚠️ The Naked Option Trap: Selling a naked call on a $20 stock might seem safe until it gets acquired at $65 and you owe $4,500 per contract. Selling naked options requires the highest options approval level for good reason.

Pattern Day Trader (PDT) Rule

If you make 4+ day trades in a 5-day rolling period in a margin account, you're flagged as a Pattern Day Trader and must maintain at least $25,000 in equity. Drop below that and you're restricted to closing positions only. This is a FINRA rule, not a broker policy.

Pro tip: Cash accounts aren't subject to PDT. But you can only trade with settled cash (T+1 for options). Many small-account traders use cash accounts to avoid the $25K minimum.

Buying Power and Leverage Ratios

Options buying power reduction depends on the position type, not a fixed ratio like stock margin:

  • Long options: 100% of premium (no leverage)
  • Defined-risk spreads: Max loss × 100 (still defined)
  • Naked options: 10-20% of underlying equivalent (massive leverage)

The key insight: buying options is cash-only (you can't lose more than you paid), but selling options unleashes margin leverage. This is why WSB loss porn almost always involves selling options or using portfolio margin.

Portfolio Margin

For accounts over $125,000, brokers may offer portfolio margin instead of Reg T. This uses risk-based calculations: a diversified portfolio of offsetting positions requires far less margin than individual positions. It can boost leverage to 6:1 or higher — and make blow-ups that much more spectacular.

Real WSB Math: The $50K → $0 Speedrun

You deposit $50,000. You sell 10 naked puts on a $50 stock, collecting $2.00 each ($2,000 total premium). Margin requirement: ~$10,000 per contract = $100,000 total. You need portfolio margin to even open this.

Stock drops to $35. Each put is $15 ITM = $15,000 loss per contract × 10 = $150,000 loss. After subtracting the $2,000 premium and your $50,000 deposit... you owe your broker $98,000.

This is not hypothetical. This happens every time there's a sharp selloff.
